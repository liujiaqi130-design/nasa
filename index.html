03 / THE EVIDENCEOPPY’S FIRST PICTURES

MY FIRST MARTIAN ADDRESS

Touchdown.
My first look around.

Opportunity landed at Meridiani Planum in January 2004. One of the first panoramas it sent home showed a dark, strange landscape from inside a small crater. Scientists chose this region partly because orbital observations had found hematite — a mineral that can form in the presence of water.

A

Look for clues

Opportunity studied rocks and soil for signs of past water. That helps scientists reconstruct what ancient Mars was like.

NASA’s Mars Exploration Rover science goals included studying rocks and soils for evidence of past water and assessing whether some ancient environments could have supported microbial life. Evidence of conditions is not evidence that life was found.

B

Meet the “blueberries”

Little rounded gray mineral spherules dotted the plains. The science team nicknamed them “blueberries.”

These small spherules are rich in hematite. Their composition and setting gave researchers clues about water-related processes in Meridiani Planum.

SCIENCE CHECK

Opportunity found evidence of past water and environments that could have been suitable for microbial life. It did not discover life.

05 / THE PASSAGE OF TIMEA REAL MISSION CHALLENGE
MARS / DUST STORM
ILLUSTRATIVE SCENE

A POWERFUL PLANET-WIDE STORM

Dust in the air.
Less light for me.

Opportunity ran on solar power. In 2018, a severe dust storm spread across Mars and blocked sunlight from reaching the rover’s solar panels. Opportunity stopped communicating with Earth on June 10.

06 / NOT FORGOTTENWHAT REMAINS

A LONG WAY PAST THE PLAN

Designed for 90 sols.
I made it to 5,111.

Opportunity’s planned mission was 90 Martian days. It worked for 5,111 sols — more than 14 Earth years — and traveled 45.16 kilometers (28.06 miles) before the mission ended.
